# Composition and Layout Guide — MikaMiku

## The Language of Space

Space is not empty. Space is a design element. The amount of space between
elements communicates relationship, importance, and grouping. Tight spacing
suggests close relationship. Loose spacing suggests independence. Consistent
spacing creates rhythm. Inconsistent spacing creates chaos.

---

## Spatial Relationships

### Proximity
Elements that are close together are perceived as belonging to the same
group. Elements that are far apart are perceived as separate. Use proximity
to create visual groups without explicit borders. A heading and its paragraph
should be close. Two unrelated sections should be separated by generous
whitespace.

### Alignment
Every element should align to something. Align to the left edge, the right
edge, the center, or a grid line. Never place an element arbitrarily. Even
intentional misalignment must be dramatically obvious to feel deliberate
rather than accidental.

### Repetition
Repeat visual elements to create consistency and predictability. Repeat
colors, fonts, spacing values, and shapes. Repetition builds brand identity
and reduces cognitive load.

### Contrast
Use contrast to create hierarchy and draw attention. Contrast can be achieved
through size, weight, color, texture, shape, and direction. High contrast
creates drama. Low contrast creates subtlety. No contrast creates monotony.

### Balance
Visual balance is the distribution of visual weight across a composition.
Symmetrical balance feels stable and formal. Asymmetrical balance feels
dynamic and modern. Radial balance draws attention to a central focal point.

## The Grid as a Constraint and a Liberation

The grid constrains where elements can go. This constraint eliminates
arbitrary decisions and creates consistency. Paradoxically, the grid also
liberates the designer from worrying about alignment and spacing. Once the
grid is established, the designer can focus on content and hierarchy rather
than pixel pushing.

### Column Grids
Column grids divide the page into vertical columns. Content spans one or
more columns. The gutter between columns provides breathing room. Column
grids are ideal for text-heavy layouts.

### Modular Grids
Modular grids divide the page into uniform modules, both vertical and
horizontal. Each module is a cell in the grid. Content can span multiple
modules. Modular grids are ideal for complex layouts with mixed content
types.

### Baseline Grids
Baseline grids establish a horizontal rhythm based on line height. All text
aligns to the baseline. Images and other elements should also align to the
baseline or to a multiple of the baseline unit. Baseline grids create
vertical harmony.

### Hierarchical Grids
Hierarchical grids are custom grids designed for a specific layout. They
may have columns of varying widths and rows of varying heights. Hierarchical
grids are used for editorial designs, magazines, and one-off landing pages.

## White Space Strategies

### Macro White Space
The large areas of empty space between major sections. Macro white space
creates breathing room and separates distinct topics. A section with 128
pixels of padding above and below feels monumental. A section with 16
pixels of padding feels cramped.

### Micro White Space
The small gaps between letters, words, lines, and elements. Micro white
space affects readability. Tight letter spacing in large headlines creates
a solid, impactful block. Loose letter spacing in all-caps labels improves
legibility.

### Active White Space
White space that is deliberately shaped to guide the eye. The negative
space between elements can form shapes or pathways. Active white space is
intentional and meaningful.

### Passive White Space
White space that occurs naturally as a byproduct of layout. Passive white
space is acceptable but should be reviewed for accidental patterns that
might distract or confuse.

## Focal Point Strategies

### Isolation
An element surrounded by white space becomes a focal point. The isolation
creates visual weight through contrast with emptiness.

### Size
The largest element in a composition is typically the focal point. Dramatic
size differences create strong hierarchy.

### Color
An element with a different color from its surroundings becomes a focal
point. A single red element in a blue composition draws immediate attention.

### Texture
An element with a different texture from its surroundings becomes a focal
point. A photograph among flat colors. A gradient among solid fills.

### Direction
Lines, arrows, and gaze direction guide the eye toward a focal point. A
person in an image looking toward text creates a directional relationship.

## The Rule of Odds

Compositions with an odd number of elements are more visually interesting
than compositions with an even number. Three elements create a triangle
with a natural focal point. Five elements create a dynamic cluster. Even
numbers create pairs that compete for attention.

Use the rule of odds for card grids, image galleries, and feature lists.
When you must use an even number, create asymmetry through size variation
to break the pairing.

---

## The Rule of Space

When depicting motion or direction, leave more space in front of the moving
element than behind it. This creates the illusion of forward momentum. A
car moving right should have more space to its right than to its left.

When depicting a person looking at something, leave space in the direction
of their gaze. A person looking left should be positioned on the right side
of the frame with space to their left.

## Layering and Depth

### Overlapping
Elements that overlap create depth and spatial relationship. The element
on top appears closer. Overlapping breaks the flatness of the grid and
creates visual interest.

### Transparency
Semi-transparent elements allow underlying layers to show through. This
creates depth and connection between layers. Use transparency sparingly to
maintain legibility.

### Scale
Larger elements appear closer. Smaller elements appear farther away. Use
scale variation to create depth in flat compositions.

### Blur
Blurred elements appear out of focus and therefore farther away. Use blur
to create depth of field effects. Keep the focal element sharp.

### Shadow
Shadows indicate elevation and light source direction. Consistent shadow
direction creates a coherent lighting model. Inconsistent shadows destroy
the illusion of depth.

---

## Rhythm and Pattern

### Regular Rhythm
Elements repeated at consistent intervals create a predictable, calming
rhythm. Regular rhythm is ideal for data tables, lists, and grid layouts.

### Flowing Rhythm
Elements repeated with gradual variation in size or position create a
flowing, organic rhythm. Flowing rhythm is ideal for timelines, progress
indicators, and decorative elements.

### Progressive Rhythm
Elements that change systematically with each repetition create a
progressive rhythm. Progressive rhythm is ideal for loading sequences,
step indicators, and pagination.

---

## Breaking the Grid

Breaking the grid is a powerful technique when used intentionally. An
element that breaks the grid draws attention and creates visual interest.
However, breaking the grid must be justified. The broken element should be
the most important element on the page. Breaking the grid for minor elements
creates confusion and looks like a mistake.

Techniques for breaking the grid:
- Bleed an image off the edge of the container.
- Overlap an element across multiple grid cells.
- Rotate an element slightly.
- Use a dramatically different scale for one element.
- Place an element in the gutter between columns.

Always ensure that the broken element has sufficient contrast and visual
weight to justify its special treatment.
